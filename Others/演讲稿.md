# Speech Script — Simple English Version

### Target: about 30 minutes, 17 slides

---

## 【Slide 1 · Title】(~1 min)

Good afternoon, everyone today I want to share my progress of impletment the MOGP on the reallife data set in the summer

Three numbers to remember: **359** sample points. **10 out of 10** — my result wins in every test. And **Q = 2** — the best model uses two scales, not one.

Let's go through the story.

---

## 【Slide 2 · Motivation】(~1.5 min)

Lets start from the background of this dataset

Cadmium, or Cd, is a metal in soil. It is bad for health. But testing for Cd is expensive.

Nickel and zinc — Ni and Zn — are cheaper to test. And often, these three metals come from the same place. Same pollution, or same rock in the ground. So they tend to appear together.

My question was simple: can I use the cheap Ni and Zn data to help predict the expensive Cd data?

Before I built any model, I checked one thing first: are these three metals really related? I found the correlation is 0.65, 0.69, and 0.70. These numbers are not small. So yes, they are related. This is the reason the rest of my project makes sense.

---

## 【Slide 3 · Looked at the Raw Data】(~1.5 min)

Before modelling, I looked at the raw numbers.

Cd and Zn had a "right-skewed" shape. This means most values are small, but a few values are very large. So I used a log transform. After that, the shape became more symmetric, more like a normal bell curve.

But Ni was different. Ni was already close to symmetric before the transform. After I applied log to Ni, it actually became a little worse.

I want to be honest about this. I still used log for all three metals, because I need them on the same scale. But for Ni, this was not a perfect choice. It was a trade-off.

---

## 【Slide 4 · Dataset and Split】(~2 min)

Now, the dataset. It is called the Jura dataset. It has 359 sample sites in the Jura mountains in Switzerland. It comes from a classic book by Goovaerts, published in 1997.

Look at this table. Cd has 259 points. I hide 100 more Cd points, to use later as a test. Ni and Zn are different — they are measured at all 359 points, with nothing hidden.

The task is: predict Cd at the 100 hidden points. This copies a real situation, where testing Cd costs more than testing Ni or Zn.

One important detail: when I compute the mean and the variance for standardization, I only use the 259 training points for Cd. Not all 359. If I used all 359, some test information would leak into training. I wanted to avoid that.

Look at the picture. At every site where Cd is missing, we still have Ni and Zn. This is the key idea. This is why a multi-output model can work here.

---

## 【Slide 5 · Three Models】(~2.5 min)

Now let me explain the three models.

The first model is an **independent GP**. GP means Gaussian Process. This is the baseline. It only uses the 259 Cd points. It cannot see Ni or Zn at all.

The second model is called **ICM**. It is a multi-output model. It uses one spatial scale. This scale is shared by all three outputs.

The third model is called **LMC**. It is also multi-output. But it can use more than one scale. Each part of the model — we call it a "latent function" — gets its own scale. In my case, I use two scales, so Q equals 2.

Here is an important relationship: ICM is just a special case of LMC, when Q equals 1.

One note about how I built this. I used a format called "long-table form." Each row has a location, a task number, and one measured value. A normal table cannot hold missing values well. But this long-table form can. The cost is: I lose a speed trick called the Kronecker speedup. Training uses 977 rows in total.

---

## 【Slide 6 · How I Checked the Result】(~2 min)

Before I trust any result, I need to check: is it real, or just luck?

I did four things.

First, I ran **10 random splits**. Each time, I chose the 100 hidden Cd points again, with a new random seed. And I standardized the data again, each time, from scratch.

Second, **paired comparison**. All three models run on the *same* split. Then I compare them using two statistical tests: a paired t-test, and a Wilcoxon test.

Third, my **metrics**. I use RMSE, MAE, and NLL. RMSE means: how big is the average prediction error. Lower is better.

Fourth, one technical setting I had to fix. The software, called GPyTorch, only uses an exact math method when the data has less than 800 points. I have 977 points. So I had to raise this limit by hand. Without this fix, the numbers become noisy and unreliable.

---

## 【Slide 7 · Main Result】(~3 min)

Now, the main result.

ICM reduces the error by **26.3 percent**, compared to the baseline. LMC reduces it by **27.3 percent**. Both numbers come from an average over 10 splits.

The p-value is less than 0.001. A p-value tells us: how likely is this result, if there is really no true difference? A small p-value means: this result is probably real, not luck. And LMC wins on all 10 out of 10 splits.

Look at this table. Independent GP has an error of 0.87. ICM brings it down to 0.64. LMC brings it down to 0.63.

Now, one question I expected. Why not just use simple linear regression?

I tried it. A plain regression, using Cd, Ni, and Zn at the same location, gives an error of 0.76. LMC gives 0.73. So most of the improvement really comes from just having Ni and Zn at the same place. The GP model only adds about 4 percent more.

But I still think the GP is worth using. Two reasons. First, linear regression cannot tell us how confident it is. The GP can. Second, linear regression completely fails where Ni and Zn are missing. But the GP can still make a reasonable guess there, using nearby points.

---

## 【Slide 8 · What Predictions Look Like】(~1.5 min)

This picture shows the predictions directly.

Each dot is one test site. The x-axis is the true Cd value. The y-axis is the predicted value. A dot on the dashed line means a perfect prediction.

Look at the three plots, left to right: independent GP, then ICM, then LMC. You can see the dots move closer to the line. This is the same improvement we just saw in the RMSE numbers, but now you can see it directly.

---

## 【Slide 9 · Same Result on All 10 Splits】(~2 min)

I want to confirm: is this improvement stable? Or did I just get lucky one time?

Here, each grey line is one random split. From independent GP to ICM, and from independent GP to LMC, both p-values are less than 0.001. And the win rate is 10 out of 10, both times.

Look at the lines. They go down from left to right. They almost never cross each other. This tells me the improvement is stable. It does not depend on one lucky split.

---

## 【Slide 10 · Where the Improvement Comes From】(~1.5 min)

Now a deeper question: where, exactly, does this improvement come from?

Across the 100 test points, the uncertainty becomes about 23 percent smaller, on average. But — and this is important — this only happens within about 0.05 kilometers of a real sample point.

This number, 0.05 kilometers, is very close to a number the model learned by itself: 0.052 kilometers. This is called a "lengthscale." It tells the model how far away two points can still affect each other.

So my idea is: the improvement comes from borrowing information at the same location. It is not simple smoothing over a large area.

---

## 【Slide 11 · Where on the Map】(~2 min)

Let's look at this on a map.

Top row: predicted Cd values, for independent GP and for LMC, and the difference between them. LMC follows the local details more closely.

Bottom row: how uncertain each model is. On the right, the percentage change. Red areas mean LMC is more confident than independent GP. Notice — red areas sit almost exactly on the sample points. In the empty areas between points, there is very little gain.

---

## 【Slide 12 · How Far the Borrowing Reaches】(~1.5 min)

This slide checks the same idea, using numbers.

The two curves — GP and LMC — cross at about 0.05 kilometers. The lengthscale the model learned is 0.052 kilometers. These two numbers are almost the same.

For reference, the average distance between neighboring points is 0.125 kilometers. The regular grid spacing is 0.25 kilometers.

Since the crossing point matches the learned lengthscale so closely, I believe the borrowing effect really is limited to about one lengthscale. It does not reach much further than that.

---

## 【Slide 13 · Two Scales, Two Meanings】(~2.5 min)

Now, something I find interesting.

Look at this table. The LMC model, with Q equals 2, learned two separate scales.

The first one is short-range. Its scale is about 50 meters. It explains 77.7 percent of the variation in Cd. Its correlation with Ni is 0.673. My guess is: this represents local pollution. Something added by human activity, close to the surface.

The second one is long-range. Its scale is about 500 meters. It explains only 22.3 percent of the variation in Cd. But its correlation with Ni is 0.941 — very close to 1. My guess is: this represents the regional geology. The natural rock and soil under a wide area.

Here is something surprising. Even though the long-range part explains only 22 percent of the variance, it actually contributes 63 percent of the shape we see in the final prediction map. So explaining variance, and having real influence on the result, are not the same thing.

---

## 【Slide 14 · A Result I Want to Say Up Front】(~2.5 min)

Now, I want to share something honestly, before anyone asks.

I expected LMC to predict better than ICM. But the test says: no, not significantly. LMC only won 5 out of 10 splits. The p-value is 0.19. This is not significant. So I cannot say LMC predicts more accurately.

But I noticed something else. Look at the table. ICM has only one correlation number: about 0.74. LMC splits this into two numbers: 0.67 and 0.94. And ICM's single number sits almost exactly between them.

My understanding is: ICM is mixing two different things into one average number. Its structure cannot separate them. This mixed number also caused an earlier mistake of mine — at first, I thought Cd's spatial correlation was weak. But that was wrong. It was just this averaging effect hiding the real structure.

---

## 【Slide 15 · Summary】(~1.5 min)

Let me summarize.

What improved: both multi-output models beat the baseline, on all 10 splits. The improvement mostly comes from having Ni and Zn measured at the same location. Compared to simple linear regression, the GP still adds value — a real uncertainty estimate, and the ability to keep predicting where data is missing.

What I found interesting: with Q equals 2, LMC separates a short-range part and a long-range part. I believe these connect to local pollution and regional geology. But I want more evidence before I say this firmly.

How the slides connect: the correlation exists — that is slide 2. Borrowing information reduces uncertainty — slides 6 to 10. Allowing two scales reveals the real structure — slide 11. But this structure does not improve prediction accuracy — I tested this honestly, and reported it, on slide 12.

---

## 【Slide 16 · What I Know, What I Don't】(~1.5 min)

Finally, I want to be clear about what I still need to learn.

First, how to properly choose Q. I only compared Q equals 1 and Q equals 2. This is the smallest possible test, to check if more than one scale exists. But choosing the *right* number of scales needs model-selection tools, like BIC or AIC. I am still learning this part.

Second, my model uses one shared noise value for all three metals. I have not checked if giving each metal its own noise value would matter.

Third, I have only tested this on one dataset. And the whole improvement depends on Ni and Zn being measured at the same location as Cd. I do not yet know what happens when they are not.
